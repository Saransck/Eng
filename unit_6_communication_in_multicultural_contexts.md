### Introduction to High-Context and Low-Context Business Cultures

> **[Illustration]:** A handshake bridging two cultural styles—traditional Asian architecture and attire focusing on relationship/harmony on the left, and modern Western corporate skyscrapers focusing on tasks, time, and charts on the right.

According to Sheposh (2025), anthropologist Edward T. Hall introduced the idea of ***high-context*** and ***low-context*** cultures to explain how people in different societies communicate. In high-context cultures such as those in many Asian and African countries, people often communicate indirectly, relying on tone, gestures, and relationships to share meaning. In contrast, low-context cultures, like the United States or many European nations, prefer direct and clear communication. People value individual achievement and focus more on explicit messages than on background or relationships. Hall's concept helps explain why misunderstandings can happen in cross-cultural communication and reminds us to be aware of different communication styles.

#### Reported speech rules:

> **1. When we report statements, we change the present tense to the past tense e.g.,**
> * $\text{present simple } (\text{S}+\text{V}_1) \longrightarrow \text{past simple } (\text{S}+\text{V}_2)$
> * $\text{present continuous } (\text{S} + \text{Is/Am/Are} + \text{V-ing}) \longrightarrow \text{past continuous } (\text{S} + \text{Was/Were} + \text{V-ing})$
> * $\text{present perfect } (\text{S} + \text{Has/Have} + \text{V}_3) \longrightarrow \text{past perfect } (\text{S} + \text{Had} + \text{V}_3)$
> * $\text{will } (+ \text{V}_1) \longrightarrow \text{would } (+ \text{V}_1)$
> * $\text{can } (+ \text{V}_1) \longrightarrow \text{could } (+ \text{V}_1)$
>
> * Backshift the tense of the original statement. This means that present simple becomes past simple, present continuous becomes past continuous, and so on. For example, she said, "I am studying", becomes "she said that she was studying."
> * Also, change modals like "can" to "could" "will" to "would" etc. For example, she said, "I can swim" becomes "she said that she could swim."

> **2. We also change pronouns, for example, "I love my job." $\longrightarrow$ You said that you loved your job, so why did you quit?**
> * **Subject Pronouns:** Change the subject pronouns to match the person speaking.
>   * **Example:** He said, "I am going." becomes He said that he was going.
>   * **Common Subject Pronouns:** I, you, we, they
> * **Possessive Pronouns:** Adjust possessive words like "my," "your," "our," and "their" to match the person being reported.

---

## Page 78

>   * **Example:** She said, "It is my pen." becomes She said it was her pen.
>   * **Common Possessive Words:** my, your, our, their
> * **Object Pronouns and Possessive Pronouns Together:** When reporting a sentence with both an object pronoun and possessive pronoun, change each to match the new subject.
>   * **Example:** She told him, "I want to give you my advice." becomes She told him that she wanted to give him her advice.
>   * **Common Object Pronouns:** me, him, her, us, them

> **3. Add if or whether for yes/no questions, for example, "Do you work here?" $\longrightarrow$ That security guard asked me if I worked here.**
> * Use "if" or "whether" to introduce the reported yes/no question. Both are correct, but "whether" is often preferred in formal writing.
> * Choose an appropriate reporting verb like "ask," "inquire," "wonder," etc., to indicate that a question was asked.
> * Change the word order: In the reported form, invert the subject and the auxiliary/modal verb.
>   * <u>Example:</u>
>     * Direct Question: "Do you like the new project?"
>     * Reported Question: He asked if I liked the new project.
> * Omit the question mark: unlike direct questions, reported yes/no questions do not end with a question mark.

*Source: adapted from Tullis, G., & Trappe, T. (2002).*

#### Working with Different Cultures in Business

> **[Illustration]:** Four professionals from diverse cultural backgrounds discussing around a table with a holographic globe labeled *"GLOBAL TEAM & INSIGHT"*.

In today's global economy, business happens across international borders. Most people use English to communicate, but the small details of how we behave vary significantly. If we do not understand cultural "rules," we might make unfair judgments or ruin our professional relationships. To be successful, you should learn about these three important areas of etiquette.

##### 1. Using Names and Titles
The way we use names can cause confusion. In many European and Latin American countries, people use formal titles like "Mr.," "Ms.," or academic titles like "Dr." to show respect for someone's hard work. However, in the UK and the U.S., people often prefer a more informal approach and use first names very quickly.
A German businessman might feel disrespected if you omit his title. But a British person might think using a title is too pompous (acting too important). The best strategy is to observe how others behave or simply ask how they would like to be addressed.

##### 2. The Meaning of a Smile
In the West, people often smile to "break the ice," but its meaning changes around the world. In the U.S., Australia, and Britain, a smile signifies openness. However, in Russia, smiling for no reason can seem insincere or even "silly."
Research shows that in Switzerland and China, people associate smiling with intelligence. But in Japan and Iran, people often think a serious face looks more capable and professional. In some places, like India, a smile might even be misinterpreted as a sign of dishonesty.

##### 3. Eye Contact and Authority
Looking someone in the eye is not the same in every culture. In the UK or the U.S., direct eye contact proves you are paying attention. However, in Japan or South Korea, it can be seen as aggressive or disrespectful. In parts of Africa and South America, looking at a superior for too long might be interpreted as a challenge to their authority.

##### Conclusion: Avoid Stereotypes
Learning about these patterns is helpful, but we should not pigeonhole people (label everyone from one country as the same). A person's behavior depends on many factors, including their age, gender, and personal experiences. Use this cultural knowledge as a guide, but always treat your business partners as individuals.
