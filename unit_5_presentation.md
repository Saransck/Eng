It is beneficial to master the following signposting phrases for professional presentation performance.

### <u>Introduction</u>

**Welcoming and greeting the audience.**
* Hello, everyone. I'd like to thank you all for coming here today.
* Good morning, everyone and welcome to my presentation. First of all, let me thank you all for coming here today.

**Introducing yourself (name, position, responsibilities)**
* Let me introduce myself. I'm *<u>Nawarat Pinthong</u>* from *<u>Kasetsart University</u>*.
* My name is *<u>Nawarat Pinthong</u>*, from *<u>the faculty of Management Sciences</u>*.

**Saying what your topic/title/subject is**
* What I'd like to present to you today is...
* As you can see on the screen, our topic today is...
* The subject/focus/topic/title of my presentation/talk/speech is...

**Presenting the outline/organization/structure of your presentation**
* I've divided my presentation into three main parts. My first point concerns...
* In my presentation I'll focus on three major issues. I'll begin / start off by... Then I'll move on to... Then / Next / After that, I'll be looking at...
* We thought it would be useful to divide our talk into three main sections. First / First of all, I'd like to give you an overview of...

### <u>Main part of the presentation (signposting)</u>

**Saying what is coming.**
* In this part of my presentation, I'd like to talk about...
* So, let me first give you a brief overview...

These steps can help you have a great vacation!</u>*

#### Focus 2: Discourse Markers for Presentations
Discourse markers, commonly known as 'linking words' or 'connectors,' act like the glue that holds your ideas together. They help different parts of your presentation flow smoothly. While they are essential for clear structure, they are used less often in casual talk, except in very formal situations.

Check the rules below.

> **1. Showing Result or Effect**
> * **Function:** Used to show the outcome, result, or logical consequence of what has just been said.
> * **Common Discourse Markers:** *therefore, consequently, as a result*
> * **Examples:**
>   * The team completed the project ahead of schedule. **Therefore,** the client renewed the contract.
>   * We didn't promote the product enough. **As a result,** sales remained low.
>   * Demand increased rapidly. **Consequently,** production had to be expanded.

> **2. Showing Contrast**
> * **Function:** Used to introduce an opposite idea, show a difference, or express an unexpected result.
> * **Common Discourse Markers:** *however, on the other hand, in contrast*
> * **Examples:**
>   * The old system was easy to use. **However,** it was not secure.
>   * Many employees prefer working from home. **On the other hand,** some feel isolated.
>   * Sales rose in Asia. **In contrast,** they fell sharply in Europe.

> **3. Adding Information**
> * **Function:** Used to add extra information or reinforce what has just been mentioned.
> * **Common Discourse Markers:** *moreover, furthermore, in addition*
> * **Examples:**
>   * The new app is easy to use. **Moreover,** it works on all devices.
>   * The program improves writing skills. **In addition,** it develops critical thinking.
>   * The company offers good benefits. **Furthermore,** it supports flexible working hours.

*Source: adapted from University of Warwick (2020).*

#### Dealing with nervousness

> **[Illustration Text]:** *AHHHHHHH!*

As American author Mark Twain famously said, "There are two types of people: those who are nervous and those who lie about it." Almost everyone feels nervous before a presentation, whether it's a big or small audience, colleagues or strangers. The key is not to avoid nervousness but to find effective ways to handle it and even use it to improve your performance.

Here are some helpful tips for reducing nervousness:

**Be prepared.** "Failing to prepare is preparing to fail." Preparation is essential for a strong presentation. Knowing exactly what you want to say and practicing it beforehand can help you feel more confident. Practice until you feel comfortable with the material so you can focus on other parts of the presentation.

**Learn to relax.** Relaxing your body can reduce nervousness. Try stretching or breathing exercises before you speak. For example, sit upright, breathe in slowly, hold for five seconds, then breathe out slowly. Relax your facial muscles by opening your eyes and mouth wide, then closing them tightly.

**Familiarize yourself with the room.** Arrive early and get to know the space where you will speak. Walk around, check that everything you need is in place, and practice with any equipment like a microphone or projector if you plan to use them.

**Get to know your audience.** If possible, greet people as they arrive. It will feel more comfortable to speak to familiar faces than to complete strangers.

**Focus on your message.** Instead of thinking about your nervousness, focus on what you want to share and how it can benefit the audience.

**Visualize success.** Picture yourself speaking confidently and imagine the audience applauding at the end. Visualizing a positive experience can make you feel more at ease.

By following these steps, you can better manage your nervousness. Remember, being a little nervous can also be helpful; it can give you the energy boost needed for an enthusiastic presentation.

*Source: adapted from Grussendorf, M. (2007)*
